# Kit vs GetResponse: newsletter-first or funnel-first

The two products are usually compared as if they were the same purchase at two prices. They are not. One is built email-first and stays narrow on purpose. The other is a wider marketing surface, and the width is the whole point of buying it.

## The difference that decides it

Kit is built around the newsletter. Automation for welcome sequences and tagging stays legible, and the product assumes email is the channel, not one channel among several.

GetResponse is built around the campaign. Email, landing pages, and simple funnels sit in one account with the pieces already connected, which saves you assembling them from three subscriptions.

The practical split: choose Kit when the list is the asset. Choose GetResponse when the list is one part of a launch.

## Pick Kit when

- You intend to write to the list every week. This is the assumption the product is built on, and it is the assumption that makes it worth paying for.
- You want automation you can still read six months later. Welcome sequences and tagging stay short enough to audit.
- You do not need pipelines, sales reporting, or a CRM. Those sit outside what Kit does, and buying it for them is a mismatch.

## Pick GetResponse when

- The plan includes landing pages and a funnel, not only a list. That is the surface you are paying for.
- You want the campaign pieces connected from the start rather than stitched together with a third tool.
- You are willing to spend the setup time. More surface means more to configure before the first send.

## The cost of the wider tool

A newsletter that only needs to send and tag rarely uses the extra half of a marketing suite. That is not a flaw in GetResponse. It is a mismatch between what it is built to do and what you actually do every month.

The reverse cost exists too: a team running a launch on a newsletter-first tool ends up paying for a landing page builder and a form tool separately, and then connecting them. Count those line items before you decide, not after.

## When to skip both

If you send four emails a year, neither product earns its subscription. A plain contact list and your existing inbox cover that volume, and migrating later is a smaller job than most people expect.

If the list is already large and the only problem is deliverability, the tool is not your problem. The content and the sending pattern are.

## Before you commit

Check which features you actually need sit on the plan you are buying. Landing pages, tagging rules, and automation steps are the three things most often used to separate tiers. Read the current pricing page rather than a comparison written last year, because tier boundaries move.

## Bottom line

Kit when you are committing to a newsletter you write to every week. GetResponse when email is one piece of a campaign with a landing page and a funnel behind it. Pick the narrow tool if the narrow job is the real job.
